# Fiction Prose Rules

Everything in `story-forge`'s `references/prose-rules.md` applies — banned
constructions, rhythm, verbs, adverbs, restatement, concrete over abstract, writing for
a voice rather than a page. This file covers what fiction adds.

## The one rule underneath the others

Still: **never write to reach a length.** The writer gets a beat, a job, and a cap, and
never a duration. Padding to length is what slop is, in fiction as much as anywhere.

## Told emotion — hard fail

The single most common failure in horror prose, and the one the linter is strictest
about.

```
She was terrified.
The house felt eerie.
A sense of dread filled the room.
He grew uneasy.
```

Each of these asserts an effect the prose has not earned. A reader who is *told* they
are frightened stops being frightened — the sentence does the feeling on their behalf,
which is exactly the job you were supposed to do.

The fix is never a better adjective. It is a detail that produces the feeling:

| Told | Caused |
|---|---|
| She was terrified | She counted the stairs on the way down. There were fourteen. There had always been thirteen |
| The silence was unsettling | The fridge had stopped. She could not remember it stopping |
| He felt watched | He moved the chair so his back was to the wall, and then moved it again |
| A sense of dread | She checked the lock twice and then a third time, and stood there with her hand on it |

The test: delete the emotion word. If the sentence no longer conveys anything, the
sentence was never doing the work.

This is fiction's equivalent of unfalsifiable attribution — the writer asserting a
conclusion rather than providing what supports it.

## Genre cliché — hard fail

```
a chill ran down her spine        heart pounded          blood ran cold
hair stood on end                 deafening silence      goosebumps
shadows danced                    something was watching  she wasn't alone
little did she know               or so they thought      never seen again
```

Every one is a writer reaching for the genre's furniture instead of the specific detail
that would land. They are also the phrases a reader's eye slides over, having seen them
a thousand times — which is the opposite of what horror needs.

Physical fear responses are real and worth writing. Write the one this character has, in
this room, not the stock one:

> Her hands were fine. That was the part she kept coming back to afterwards — her hands
> were completely steady, and she could not make herself turn around.

## What horror prose actually does

**Specific beats general.** "Something moved in the dark" is nothing. "The coat on the
back of the door was on the floor" is something, because the reader has that door.

**The wrong detail, not the scary one.** Dread comes from a small thing being wrong in a
way that has no explanation. Blood is an event; a chair moved four inches is a problem.

**Withhold the shape.** The moment the audience can picture the thing, they stop
supplying their own version, and theirs was better. This is the prose-level version of
the ledger's deliberate ambiguity.

**Ordinary language for extraordinary events.** Escalating the vocabulary tells the
reader you are trying. Flat, plain sentences about impossible things are worse — in the
right way.

**Silence and rhythm.** Short sentences after long ones land hardest, and horror needs
the landing more than most. The rhythm checks in the linter matter more here, not less.

## Point of view discipline

Fix POV and tense in the brief and hold them. Slipping from close third into omniscient
to deliver information the character could not have is the most common structural cheat
in fiction, and it dissolves the tension immediately — an audience that knows more than
the character is watching, not worried.

If the reader needs to know something the POV character cannot, that is a canon and
ledger problem, not a prose problem. Fix it upstream.

## What the linter cannot see

It catches told emotion and stock phrases. It cannot tell you the scare does not land,
that the pacing sags in the middle, or that the ending explains too much.

That is the cold reader's job, and the cold reader is the only auditor measuring the
thing that actually matters. A clean lint means the auditors start from a clean file.
